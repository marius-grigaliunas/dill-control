# The story

**Parked, not decided: the vertical slice comes first.** Nothing here is a
decision record, nothing is in the slice, and any of it may change when the slice
is played. The slice is onboarding and three working contracts
(`docs/decisions/0051`). The story comes after. The only parts of it the slice
touches are the opening (`0054`) and the clone rule (`0053`), both of which are
decided on their own.

*Direction settled 27 October 2026; the debt, the endings and the rival
written down 29 October.*

## Why no pivot

One procedural planet, one nest and one contract type gets boring fast. That
raised a pivot to a story-driven game. The answer is **no pivot**: a story is
layered over curated contracts built on the existing procedural tech. A fully
authored story game is too costly for one person at 10 to 20 hours a week, and
it would waste the replayable destruction sandbox the game already is.

Rejected on the way:

- **A mystery plot.** The company causing the infestation is a one-sentence
  twist, and nobody plays a horde shooter to solve anything.
- **Player-like AI rivals sharing your planets.** Believable player AI is too
  expensive, and a rival clearing the nest steals the very thing the player came
  for.
- **Taming.** Too much complexity, found in the 28 October search for emergent
  systems that add depth without another year of work.

## The spine is debt

The player starts the game owing the company a large sum for the ship, the
cloning contract and the starter gear. The balance is printed at the bottom of
every invoice. Contracts pay it down. Deaths and failures push it up through
clone fees and a billed drop and ammo, with a **cap** so no player is ever
buried. The debt is both the narrative and the progress bar.

**Unsolved: how does the player buy shop upgrades (`0052`) while always in
debt?** Candidates, none chosen: upgrades are paid from the invoice's surplus
before the debt; the shop sells on credit and the debt grows; the company lends
gear that is repossessed at the end. The slice sidesteps this: the invoice shows
no debt balance, and money is only what the shop spends (`0053`).

## The theme is show, don't tell

The cucumbers are living, frightened beings that only fight when provoked. The
company and the player are the real bad guys, and the game never says so:
attentive players decide for themselves. This builds on 0041 (the hive never
strikes first) and 0050 (the cucumbers are innocent). What carries it:

- the scared and angry faces (0050);
- the hive that only wakes when touched, so the player always strikes first;
- Tanks rushing to defend a damaged vine within a tight radius. **Parked:** they
  still throw and punch, so the guarding puts them in the player's way, but
  under 0041 harm wakes the whole colony, so a local guard has nothing to add
  yet. Revisit if the wake ever becomes partial.
- invoice wording that grows colder across the campaign, from specimens to units
  to biomass.

There are **no young cucumbers**. They ripen unconscious on the vine and drop
fully grown, so the pods and vines are the unborn, and no child-protecting
behaviour exists. Cornichons are not children. Corpse-carrying was dropped as
unclear and too costly with shader-drawn capsules.

## Two endings: a direction only

The player either **accepts the company's renewal** and gets new gear such as a
mech, or **turns on the company**. The cheap way to build the rebel ending is to
unleash the horde on the company, for example by planting a queen or a seed pod
on its headquarters or main mining world, rather than building human enemies
and interiors. This needs more work before it is a decision.

## The recurring rival

A **scripted boss**, not a player-imitating AI. He shows up on some contracts
with a radio taunt and uses the horde against you: he wakes nests and blows up
your grapple anchors. He tracks your favourite weapon, movement and fighting
spot, and brings **one visible counter each time he returns**, such as flares
against rocket spam or cutting vines against grapple use. He bails out instead
of dying until the finale, comes back scarred, and takes your paycheck if he
wins. A small cast of recurring characters may follow later.

**He stays apart from Warner Bros' patented Nemesis system:** one authored
rival, no generated hierarchy, and genuinely different mechanics rather than
renamed ones. If the design drifts toward a ranked, procedurally generated cast
of enemies who remember the player, it has drifted too far.
